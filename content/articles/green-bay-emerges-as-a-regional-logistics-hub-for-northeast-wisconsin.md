Warehouse and distribution development around Green Bay has accelerated over the past several years, positioning the city as a logistics alternative to larger, pricier hubs in Milwaukee and the Twin Cities. Developers point to a combination of factors driving the shift: available industrial land, highway access via Interstates 43 and 41, and a working port that gives certain shippers a bulk-cargo option unavailable further inland.

Brokers who track industrial real estate in northeast Wisconsin describe steady absorption of new warehouse space over recent leasing cycles, with tenants ranging from regional food distributors to e-commerce fulfillment operators drawn by lower land and construction costs relative to markets closer to Chicago.

## Highway Access Meets Port Capacity

Green Bay's logistics appeal rests on a somewhat unusual combination for a city its size: strong interstate connectivity paired with a working commercial port on Lake Michigan's Green Bay waterway. That port handles bulk commodities including coal, limestone, and agricultural products, giving certain manufacturers and processors an alternative to all-truck or all-rail supply chains.

Distribution companies serving northeast and central Wisconsin increasingly cite drive-time efficiency as a core rationale for locating in Green Bay rather than Milwaukee. A facility in the Green Bay area can reach much of Wisconsin, the Upper Peninsula of Michigan, and parts of northern Illinois within a single day's drive, a radius that logistics planners say is increasingly valuable as retailers and manufacturers push for faster last-mile delivery times.

## Paper Industry Legacy Shapes the Workforce

Green Bay's long history as a paper manufacturing center has left behind a workforce accustomed to industrial shift work, heavy equipment operation, and logistics coordination, skills that translate directly into warehouse and distribution employment. Several logistics employers in the area say this labor pool, combined with a lower cost of living than Milwaukee or Chicago, has made hiring for warehouse and driver positions comparatively easier than in more competitive metro labor markets.

That said, employers caution that competition for CDL-holding drivers remains intense across the entire Upper Midwest, and wage growth in logistics roles has outpaced overall regional wage growth as companies compete for a limited pool of qualified drivers and equipment operators.

## Investment Momentum and Local Incentives

Local economic development authorities have actively courted logistics investment, offering tax-increment financing districts and infrastructure improvements around key industrial corridors. Officials describe the strategy as an intentional diversification play, aimed at reducing the local economy's historical reliance on paper and packaging manufacturing by building out a broader industrial and logistics base.

Developers active in the market say speculative warehouse construction, buildings built without a signed tenant in hand, has picked up modestly after a slower stretch, a sign that investors are betting on continued demand rather than simply responding to existing leases. Even so, most new projects remain smaller in scale than the mega-warehouses common around larger metro areas, reflecting both land availability and a tenant base weighted toward regional rather than national distribution operations.

## What Comes Next

Industry observers expect Green Bay's logistics sector to keep growing incrementally rather than experiencing a single transformative project. The city's combination of highway access, port capacity, and available industrial land gives it durable structural advantages, but its growth trajectory will likely remain tied closely to broader regional retail and manufacturing demand rather than the kind of national e-commerce mega-hub status seen in larger metro areas.

For northeast Wisconsin's broader economy, the logistics buildout represents a meaningful complement to the region's manufacturing base, providing a new category of industrial employment that does not depend solely on the health of any single sector like papermaking or food processing.
