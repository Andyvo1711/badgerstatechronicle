Several colleges within the Wisconsin Technical College System are expanding their welding and metal fabrication programs, adding evening sections, new equipment, and additional instructor positions to meet what administrators describe as consistent, sometimes overwhelming demand from local manufacturers. Waitlists for welding certificate programs have become common at a number of campuses in recent years, a dynamic system officials say reflects both strong student interest and a genuine shortage of skilled welders across the state's manufacturing sector.

Wisconsin's manufacturing base, concentrated heavily in the southeastern and Fox Valley regions but present throughout the state, has long relied on technical college graduates to fill entry-level and mid-level production roles. Industry groups have warned for years of a coming wave of retirements among experienced welders and machinists, a demographic shift that has added urgency to efforts to train replacements.

## Waitlists and Expansion

"We've had semesters where we simply couldn't accommodate everyone who wanted into the welding program," said a program director at one of the technical colleges expanding its offerings. "That's a good problem to have in terms of interest, but it means we've had to get creative about capacity, whether that's adding evening cohorts or finding funding for more booths and equipment."

The expansions include additional welding booths, new certification tracks aligned with specific manufacturing processes like robotic and pipe welding, and partnerships with local employers who provide equipment donations or paid apprenticeship placements for students still completing coursework. Several colleges have also added flexible scheduling options aimed at working adults looking to switch careers into the trades, a population system officials say has grown as a share of enrollment in recent years.

## Pay and Career Pathways

Part of the appeal for students, program administrators say, is a clear and relatively fast pathway from enrollment to a well-paying job, particularly compared to the multi-year timelines and accumulated debt associated with a four-year degree. Starting wages for certified welders in Wisconsin's manufacturing sector have risen in recent years amid the broader worker shortage, making the trade an increasingly attractive option for students weighing their post-high-school options.

"I looked at the cost of a four-year degree and then looked at what welders in my area were making, and it wasn't a hard decision," said a student currently enrolled in a welding certificate program who previously worked in retail. "I'll be done in under two years with a lot less debt."

## Employer Involvement

Local manufacturers have played an active role in shaping curriculum updates, with several sitting on advisory boards that help technical colleges keep pace with evolving industry equipment and techniques. Employers say this close relationship helps ensure graduates arrive with skills that translate directly to the shop floor, reducing the on-the-job training burden companies would otherwise have to absorb themselves.

"We'd rather invest time now, helping shape what students learn, than spend months retraining someone after they're hired," said a hiring manager at a Wisconsin manufacturing company that regularly recruits from technical college welding programs.

## Looking Ahead

System officials say they expect demand for welding and fabrication training to remain strong for the foreseeable future, driven both by manufacturing employment trends and by growing public interest in trade careers as an alternative to traditional four-year college paths. Additional capacity expansions are under consideration at several more campuses, contingent on state funding and continued employer partnership support.
