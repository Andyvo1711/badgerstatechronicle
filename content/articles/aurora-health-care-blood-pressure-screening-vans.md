Parked outside a grocery store on Milwaukee's north side on a recent Saturday, an Aurora Health Care mobile screening van drew a steady line of shoppers curious enough to step inside for a free blood pressure check. The pilot program, now operating at a rotating set of locations across southeastern Wisconsin, is aimed at a condition that public health officials describe as both common and commonly ignored: high blood pressure.

Hypertension often produces no obvious symptoms, which is part of why health officials estimate that a substantial share of Wisconsin adults with the condition remain undiagnosed or inconsistently managed. Aurora's mobile van initiative is designed to catch cases in places people already visit regularly, rather than waiting for patients to schedule a dedicated doctor's appointment.

## Meeting People Where They Are

The screening vans, staffed by nurses and community health workers, rotate between grocery stores, pharmacies, churches, and community centers in neighborhoods identified as having lower rates of routine primary care access. Each visit takes only a few minutes, with staff recording blood pressure readings and offering basic guidance on when a result warrants a follow-up appointment.

"A lot of people tell us it's been years since they've had their blood pressure checked," said a community health worker who staffs the van several days a week. "They're not avoiding the doctor out of neglect. It's often a scheduling problem, a transportation problem, or they just don't have a primary care provider at all."

Readings that fall into concerning ranges trigger an on-the-spot conversation about next steps, and staff can help uninsured or underinsured patients connect with sliding-scale clinic options in the Aurora network. Program organizers say the goal is not to replace ongoing primary care but to serve as an entry point for patients who have fallen out of the healthcare system entirely.

## A Preventable Driver of Serious Illness

Uncontrolled hypertension is a leading contributor to heart attacks, strokes, and kidney disease, conditions that are both costly to treat and, in many cases, preventable with early management. A physician involved in designing the screening program said the economics of prevention make the investment worthwhile even though the vans themselves generate no direct billing revenue.

"One stroke hospitalization costs far more than years of screening and medication management," the physician said. "If we can catch even a small percentage of undiagnosed cases early, the downstream savings, in both health outcomes and cost, are significant."

## Building Trust in Underserved Neighborhoods

Program organizers say building trust has been as important as the clinical work itself. Several of the neighborhoods targeted by the van program have historically reported lower engagement with the traditional healthcare system, shaped in part by past experiences with inaccessible or unaffordable care. Staff say returning to the same locations on a predictable schedule, rather than a one-time event, has helped build familiarity over time.

## Expansion Plans

Aurora Health Care officials say they are evaluating data from the pilot's first several months to determine which locations produce the highest rates of new diagnoses, information that will guide where additional vans are deployed later this year. They are also exploring adding basic diabetes screening to the van's services, given how often the two conditions occur together.

For now, the vans represent a small but tangible experiment in shrinking the distance between people and the healthcare system, one grocery store parking lot at a time.
