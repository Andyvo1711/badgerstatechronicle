Door County's tourism-driven economy is heading into peak summer season with booking levels that lodging operators describe as tracking ahead of recent years, continuing a sustained recovery and growth trend for the peninsula's hospitality industry. Hotels, resorts, and short-term rentals across the county's waterfront communities report strong advance bookings for July and August, historically the region's busiest months.

Tourism remains the single largest driver of Door County's local economy, supporting a dense network of restaurants, retail shops, orchards, and lodging properties that depend heavily on a compressed summer season to generate the majority of their annual revenue. Local business owners describe a delicate balancing act: maximizing revenue during peak months while managing a workforce and infrastructure base sized for a much smaller off-season population.

## Workforce Housing Remains the Binding Constraint

Despite strong visitor demand, tourism business owners across Door County consistently identify workforce housing availability as the primary constraint limiting their ability to fully staff operations during peak season. Seasonal employees, many of whom come from outside the immediate area, face a severely limited supply of affordable short-term housing in a market where available units increasingly compete with lucrative vacation rental income.

Some larger resorts have responded by developing or leasing dedicated employee housing, an investment that adds to operating costs but has become increasingly necessary to reliably staff summer operations. Smaller, independently owned businesses without the capital to invest in dedicated housing describe more persistent staffing challenges, sometimes limiting operating hours or scaling back service offerings during peak demand periods as a direct result.

## A Shifting Visitor Profile

Door County tourism officials describe a visitor base that has evolved somewhat in recent years, with growing interest in shoulder-season travel during May, September, and October, periods that have traditionally seen much lighter visitor traffic than the core summer months. Local tourism promotion organizations have actively marketed fall foliage, wine and cider tourism, and off-season outdoor recreation as ways to extend the county's tourism revenue beyond the traditional summer peak.

This shoulder-season growth, while still modest relative to summer volumes, offers a meaningful economic benefit for local businesses by smoothing revenue somewhat across a longer operating window and potentially supporting more stable year-round employment for at least a portion of the tourism workforce.

## Balancing Growth With Community Character

Local officials and business leaders describe an ongoing tension between capturing continued tourism growth and preserving the small-town character that draws many visitors to Door County in the first place. Debates over short-term rental regulation have become increasingly prominent in several Door County communities, as local residents raise concerns about vacation rentals reducing the availability of long-term housing stock for year-round residents and seasonal workers alike.

Several municipalities within the county have implemented or considered short-term rental permitting systems aimed at balancing continued tourism revenue against housing availability concerns, reflecting a broader challenge facing popular tourist destinations nationally as short-term rental platforms have reshaped local housing markets.

## Economic Stakes for the Broader Region

Beyond Door County itself, the peninsula's tourism strength ripples outward, supporting agricultural producers, including the county's well-known cherry and apple orchards, along with regional transportation and logistics businesses that serve the tourism supply chain. Local economic development officials describe Door County tourism as an increasingly important contributor to the broader northeastern Wisconsin economy, complementing the region's more traditional manufacturing and paper industry base.

As the summer season builds toward its peak, tourism officials remain optimistic about continued visitor growth, while acknowledging that sustaining that growth long-term will require meaningful progress on the workforce housing challenges that increasingly constrain how much business the county's hospitality sector can actually capture.
