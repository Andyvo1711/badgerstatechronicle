The University of Wisconsin–Green Bay has completed a significant renovation of its main library, reworking much of the ground floor into what administrators are calling a learning commons: a mix of group study rooms, an expanded tutoring center, and a small digital media studio available to students across all majors. The project reflects a broader trend among university libraries nationally, which have increasingly shifted away from being primarily quiet repositories of print materials toward serving as flexible academic hubs.

University officials say the renovation was driven by years of student feedback indicating that the library's traditional layout, dominated by rows of silent study carrels, no longer matched how most students actually wanted to use the space. Group project work, tutoring sessions, and multimedia coursework had increasingly been pushed into hallways, dorm lounges, or off-campus coffee shops due to a lack of appropriate space on campus.

## From Stacks to Studio Space

"Students still want quiet space to study alone, and we've preserved a good amount of that upstairs," said a UW-Green Bay library administrator overseeing the renovation. "But downstairs, we've essentially rebuilt around the reality that a lot of learning now happens in groups, and a lot of assignments now involve video, audio, or design work that needs different kinds of space and equipment."

The new digital media studio includes recording equipment, video editing stations, and green screen capability, available for checkout by students working on class projects, campus media organizations, or personal creative work. Early reservations for the space have skewed heavily toward communications and business students working on presentation projects, though librarians say they've also seen interest from science students producing lab presentation videos and education majors building teaching materials.

## Expanded Tutoring and Academic Support

The renovation also relocated and expanded the university's tutoring center into the library building itself, a change officials say was intended to reduce the friction of students needing to travel across campus between study space and academic support services. Tutoring center staff report that foot traffic has increased noticeably since the co-location, particularly for walk-in help sessions that previously required more deliberate planning to access.

"When the tutoring center was in a separate building, we'd sometimes lose students who just didn't have the fifteen extra minutes to walk over," said a peer tutoring coordinator. "Now it's just downstairs from where they're already studying."

## Funding and Timeline

The project was funded through a combination of state capital funding and a fundraising campaign aimed at alumni and regional donors, many of whom recalled the library's more traditional layout from their own time as students. Construction took place in phases over roughly eighteen months, with library services continuing in reduced form throughout, an arrangement students describe as occasionally inconvenient but ultimately manageable.

## Student Response

Reaction from students has been largely positive, particularly among those who rely on group study for coursework in the university's more collaborative programs, including several of its environmental science and business offerings. Some students who preferred the library's quieter, more traditional atmosphere expressed mild nostalgia for the old layout, though most said they've adjusted to the new configuration.

University officials say they view the renovated library as a long-term investment in student success, arguing that flexible, well-equipped study space has become as important to student retention as more visible investments like new dormitories or athletic facilities.
