A growing number of community and technical colleges across Wisconsin are rolling out short-term manufacturing credential programs designed to move workers into entry-level factory and production jobs within a matter of months rather than years. The push reflects sustained pressure from regional manufacturers who say traditional two-year associate degree programs, while valuable, don't always match the urgency of their hiring needs.

The new offerings typically run between eight and sixteen weeks and focus on foundational skills such as basic machine operation, workplace safety certification, quality control processes, and blueprint reading, rather than the more comprehensive coursework found in longer degree programs. Colleges say the shorter format is aimed at workers who need to start earning quickly, including those transitioning out of declining industries or entering the workforce after a period of unemployment.

## Meeting Workers Where They Are

"Not everyone can commit to two years of school before they start earning a paycheck," said a workforce development director at one of the colleges launching a new short-term program. "These credentials are designed to get someone employable fast, with the understanding that many will continue their education later, often with employer support, once they're established in a job."

Several programs have been designed in direct consultation with local manufacturers, who in some cases have committed to interviewing or hiring graduates directly upon completion. This kind of employer buy-in, college officials say, has been critical to building trust among prospective students who might otherwise be skeptical of a credential's real-world value.

## A Response to Persistent Labor Shortages

Wisconsin manufacturers have reported chronic difficulty filling entry-level production roles in recent years, a challenge compounded by an aging workforce and competition from other industries offering similar wages with less physically demanding work. Short-term credential programs are seen by many college administrators as a lower-barrier entry point that can help stabilize the talent pipeline while also serving as a stepping stone toward longer academic programs.

"We've seen students come through an eight-week program, get hired, and then come back a year later to start working on an associate degree because their employer is now paying for it," said a program coordinator at a Wisconsin technical college. "That's really the ideal pathway we're trying to build."

## Funding and Accessibility

Many of the new programs are supported through a mix of state workforce development grants and employer contributions, which colleges say has allowed them to keep tuition low or, in some cases, tuition-free for eligible students. Officials note that removing upfront cost barriers has been essential to reaching workers who might otherwise be unable to take time away from existing jobs or family responsibilities to pursue training.

## Student Perspectives

Participants in the new programs describe a mix of motivations, from career changes prompted by layoffs to a desire for more stable, better-paying work than what they'd found in retail or food service. "I didn't think I'd be working in manufacturing at this point in my life, but the program was fast, it was affordable, and the job offer came within a couple weeks of finishing," said a recent graduate of a short-term credential program in central Wisconsin.

## What Comes Next

College and workforce development officials say they expect to continue expanding short-term credential offerings across additional manufacturing subfields, including logistics and supply chain roles, as employer demand and state funding allow.
