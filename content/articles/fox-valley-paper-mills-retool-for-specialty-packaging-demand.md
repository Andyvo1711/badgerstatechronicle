The Fox Valley region's paper industry, long a defining economic pillar for communities stretching from Appleton to Neenah, continues to reinvent itself as demand for traditional printing and writing paper keeps shrinking. Mills that once ran lines dedicated to office paper and publication stock are increasingly retooling toward packaging materials, tissue products, and specialty grades tied to e-commerce shipping demand.

Industry veterans describe the shift as a continuation of a transition that began well over a decade ago, as digital media steadily eroded demand for printed communication. What has changed more recently is the scale and pace of capital investment mills are willing to commit toward packaging conversions, betting that the growth in online retail and shipping will continue supporting demand for corrugated and specialty packaging paper.

## From Office Paper to Shipping Boxes

Several Fox Valley facilities have converted or partially converted production lines that once produced coated printing paper into lines capable of producing containerboard and packaging-grade paper. The capital costs of these conversions are substantial, often running into tens of millions of dollars per line, but mill operators describe the investment as necessary to remain viable as legacy paper grades continue their long decline.

Tissue and away-from-home paper products, items like napkins, paper towels, and commercial-grade tissue, represent another growth segment that several regional producers have leaned into, citing more stable long-term demand trends compared with printing paper. Unlike packaging paper, which competes partly on commodity pricing, tissue products allow for more brand differentiation and, in some cases, better margins.

## Workforce Transition Alongside Technology Change

The shift in product mix has also reshaped the skills mills need from their workforce. Modern packaging and tissue lines rely heavily on automation and process control systems, requiring operators with technical training that goes well beyond the manual skills historically associated with paper mill work. Local technical colleges in the Fox Valley have adjusted curricula to reflect this shift, building out programs specifically focused on industrial process technology and mill automation.

Union representatives and mill managers alike describe a workforce in transition, one where retiring employees who spent careers on legacy paper grades are being replaced, where possible, by younger workers trained on newer automated systems. That transition has not been without friction, particularly in communities where paper mill employment has historically provided stable, well-paying jobs without requiring a four-year degree.

## Energy Costs and Environmental Investment

Paper manufacturing remains an energy-intensive process, and Fox Valley mills have invested heavily in efficiency upgrades and, in some cases, on-site energy generation to manage costs. Several facilities have pursued combined heat and power systems that improve energy efficiency while also reducing emissions, investments that mill operators frame both as cost management and as necessary steps to meet evolving environmental regulations.

Water usage and wastewater treatment also remain significant considerations given the Fox Valley's historical reliance on the Fox River for industrial processes. Mills operating in the region continue to invest in treatment technology, partly in response to regulatory requirements and partly to manage reputational and community relations considerations tied to the river's ongoing environmental recovery.

## A Region Still Defined by Paper, But Differently

Despite the decline in traditional paper grades, the Fox Valley remains one of the most paper-industry-dense regions in the country, and packaging and tissue conversions have allowed many mills to preserve a meaningful share of local employment even as the specific products rolling off their lines have changed dramatically. Economic development officials in the region describe the transition as evidence that the area's decades of accumulated papermaking expertise remains a genuine competitive advantage, provided mills continue investing in the equipment and training needed to compete in packaging and tissue markets rather than clinging to legacy product lines with structurally declining demand.
