Superintendents in rural districts across northern and western Wisconsin say they are heading into another school year with unfilled teaching positions, a persistent challenge that has forced some schools to combine classes, cut elective offerings, or rely more heavily on long-term substitutes than administrators would prefer. The shortages are most acute in special education, science, and world language positions, fields where rural districts say they struggle to compete with the salaries and amenities offered by larger suburban and urban districts.

The staffing challenge isn't new, but district leaders describe it as having intensified in recent years, driven by a combination of factors: fewer college students entering education programs, competition from other states and higher-paying districts, and the basic difficulty of attracting young teachers to communities that may be an hour or more from the nearest larger town.

## A Widening Gap

"We can offer a genuinely good place to raise a family, small class sizes, a real sense of community," said a superintendent of a small district in northwestern Wisconsin. "What we can't always offer is the salary that a district near Madison or Milwaukee can offer, and for a lot of new teachers carrying student loan debt, that matters a lot."

Special education positions have proven especially difficult to fill, a trend mirrored in rural districts nationally. Several superintendents said they've had to rely on shared staffing arrangements with neighboring districts or contracted services through regional education agencies just to meet legal requirements for services to students with disabilities.

## Creative Solutions, Imperfect Fixes

Districts have tried a range of strategies to cope, including "grow your own" programs that identify local high school students interested in teaching and support them through college with the expectation, though not requirement, that they'll return to teach in their home district. Some districts have also expanded partnerships with nearby technical colleges and universities offering rural-focused teacher preparation tracks, hoping to build a more direct pipeline into hard-to-staff communities.

"We've had some real success stories with grow-your-own students who come back after college because this is home for them," said a human resources administrator for a rural school district cooperative. "But it's a slow pipeline. It doesn't solve a vacancy you have right now in September."

In the meantime, some districts have turned to virtual instruction for specialized courses like upper-level science or less commonly taught world languages, partnering with regional education service agencies to give students access to instruction even when a qualified teacher isn't available locally. Superintendents describe this as a reasonable stopgap but not a substitute for an in-person teacher building relationships with students day to day.

## Compensation and State Support

District leaders have pushed for increased state funding specifically targeted at rural teacher compensation and housing incentives, arguing that without addressing the fundamental pay gap, other interventions will only partially address the problem. State lawmakers have debated various rural education funding proposals in recent sessions, though rural superintendents say progress has been slower than the urgency of the situation warrants.

"Every year we don't fill a position, it's kids who lose out," said one rural superintendent. "We need solutions on a faster timeline than the ones currently being discussed."

For now, many rural districts are heading into the new school year relying on a mix of long-term substitutes, shared staff, and virtual instruction to cover gaps, while continuing to advocate for longer-term fixes at the state level.
