Antoine Braddock can point to a city-owned lot on Milwaukee's north side and recall, almost lot by lot, which buildings once stood there before decades of disinvestment left the block scattered with vacant parcels. It is knowledge he has put to direct use as founder and president of Cream City Community Partners, a Milwaukee-based community development firm that has redeveloped more than two dozen vacant or underused properties into affordable housing, small business space, and community facilities over the past twelve years.

Braddock grew up in the same north side neighborhood where his company now does much of its work, and has said watching the area lose population and investment throughout his childhood shaped his eventual career path. After earning a graduate degree in urban planning, he spent several years working for a Milwaukee nonprofit housing developer before deciding he could move faster and take on more ambitious projects by building his own development firm, one structured to blend private financing with the community engagement practices typical of nonprofit developers.

## A Different Kind of Development Firm

Cream City Community Partners occupies something of a hybrid space in Milwaukee's development landscape, pursuing projects with a social mission while operating as a for-profit company that pays market-rate returns to its investors. Braddock has said this structure lets the company move faster than nonprofit developers often can, while still prioritizing community input and affordability requirements that many purely commercial developers skip. "We're not a charity, and we're not a typical developer either," Braddock said at a recent panel on Milwaukee's neighborhood revitalization efforts. "We're trying to prove those two things aren't as far apart as people assume."

Every Cream City Community Partners project begins with a series of community input sessions before any design work starts, a practice Braddock instituted after an early project drew criticism from neighborhood residents who felt excluded from decisions about their own block. He has since built long-term relationships with several north side and near south side neighborhood associations, treating their input as a required part of the development process rather than a formality.

## Financing Difficult Projects

Braddock has become known in Milwaukee development circles for his ability to stack complex financing packages, combining low-income housing tax credits, historic preservation credits, city land grants, and private investment to make projects financially viable in neighborhoods where market-rate financing alone would not support new construction. He has said this financial complexity is often the real barrier to community development work, more so than construction costs or community opposition. "The building part is usually the easy part," he said. "The financing puzzle is what kills most projects before they start."

## Measuring Success Beyond Square Footage

Cream City Community Partners tracks outcomes beyond typical development metrics, including how many redevelopment projects hire local contractors and how many affordable units go to residents who previously lived in the surrounding neighborhood. Braddock has pushed for local hiring requirements on his construction contracts, arguing that development without local economic participation risks displacing the very residents it claims to help.

## Looking at Milwaukee's Broader Challenges

Braddock has increasingly taken on a public advocacy role alongside his development work, testifying before Milwaukee's Common Council on vacant property policy and serving on a mayoral task force focused on neighborhood investment strategy. He remains focused on the city's north side and near south side, resisting suggestions that Cream City Community Partners expand into faster-growing suburban markets. "There's still more vacant lots on the north side than I'll get to in my career," he said. "That's where the work is."
