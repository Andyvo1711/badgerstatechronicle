Nathaniel Osei has heard every joke about Green Bay being a one-industry, one-team town, and he has built much of his career trying to prove that assumption outdated. As founder and chief executive of Titletown Data Systems, a data infrastructure and cloud services company headquartered a few miles from Lambeau Field, Osei has spent the past eight years arguing that Green Bay possesses the workforce and cost advantages to support a genuine technology sector, one not dependent on football tourism or the paper and manufacturing industries that have historically defined the region's economy.

Osei's background is not local. He moved to Green Bay in 2015 after several years working as a systems architect for a Chicago-based data center company, drawn by a combination of family ties and what he saw as an underpriced opportunity: commercial real estate and operating costs in Green Bay ran dramatically lower than in Chicago or Minneapolis, while the region's manufacturing base meant local companies increasingly needed sophisticated data infrastructure and cloud migration services that no local provider was offering.

## Finding an Underserved Market

Titletown Data Systems began by serving the data infrastructure needs of Green Bay-area paper and manufacturing companies, many of which were still running critical operations on aging on-premise servers with no local vendor equipped to manage a modernization project. Osei's first major contract came from a mid-sized paper products manufacturer looking to migrate its production planning systems to cloud infrastructure, a project that took nearly a year and became, in Osei's telling, the proof of concept that convinced other regional manufacturers Titletown could handle complex technical work without requiring them to hire a Chicago or Milwaukee-based consultancy.

The company has since expanded well beyond its manufacturing roots, building a managed cloud services division that now serves clients across healthcare, logistics, and financial services throughout northeastern Wisconsin. Osei has structured Titletown Data Systems around long-term managed service contracts rather than one-off consulting projects, a model he says provides more predictable revenue and lets the company invest further in its own infrastructure and staff training.

## Building a Local Tech Talent Pipeline

Perhaps Osei's most consistent public advocacy has centered on workforce development, arguing that Green Bay's technology sector cannot grow without a local pipeline of trained software and infrastructure talent. He helped establish a coding bootcamp partnership with a regional technical college aimed at retraining workers from declining manufacturing roles into technology careers, and Titletown Data Systems has hired more than a dozen bootcamp graduates directly into junior technical roles.

## Changing the Narrative Around Green Bay

Osei has become a visible participant in regional economic development conversations, serving on a Greater Green Bay Chamber of Commerce technology committee focused on attracting additional tech employers to the area. He has pushed back publicly against characterizations of Green Bay as solely a football town, pointing to Titletown Data Systems' growth, along with a handful of other technology firms that have emerged in the area, as evidence of a broader shift underway.

## Staying Independent Through Growth

Despite acquisition interest from larger managed services providers looking to establish a northeastern Wisconsin presence, Osei has kept Titletown Data Systems independently owned, wary that a sale would shift decision-making away from the region entirely. "Every company that gets acquired and moved somewhere else is a company Green Bay doesn't get credit for building," Osei said. "I want people to see what got built here, not just what got bought."
