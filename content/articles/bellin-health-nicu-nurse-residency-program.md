Inside the neonatal intensive care unit at Bellin Health in Green Bay, a new group of nurses is spending their first year on the job under a structured residency program built specifically for the demands of caring for critically ill and premature infants. The initiative, launched this spring, is meant to address a staffing shortfall that has left NICU shifts stretched thin across northeastern Wisconsin.

Unlike a general nursing orientation, the NICU residency pairs new graduates with experienced preceptors for an extended mentorship period, layering in specialized training on topics like ventilator management for premature lungs, precise medication dosing for infants who may weigh under two pounds, and how to support parents navigating one of the most stressful experiences of their lives.

## A Specialty Facing Acute Shortages

Neonatal nursing has long been considered one of the more difficult specialties to staff, requiring skills that most general nursing programs touch on only briefly. Bellin Health's NICU nursing director said the unit had relied heavily on recruiting experienced nurses from other hospitals, a strategy that became increasingly difficult as health systems across the state competed for the same limited pool of specialists.

"We were essentially fishing in the same small pond as everyone else," the director said. "Building our own pipeline, starting with new graduates who want this specialty from day one, felt like the more sustainable path."

The residency program accepts a small cohort of new nursing graduates each year, selected in part based on clinical rotations they completed in maternal and infant care during nursing school. Participants commit to an extended orientation period, roughly twice the length of a standard unit orientation, before taking on a full independent patient assignment.

## Supporting Families Through Crisis

Clinical skill is only part of what the residency emphasizes. New NICU nurses also receive training in family-centered care, including how to communicate with parents during high-stress moments and how to help families become active participants in their infant's care, from skin-to-skin holding to learning feeding cues before discharge.

A nurse currently completing the residency said the emotional preparation has been as valuable as the technical training. "You're not just caring for a baby, you're caring for an entire family that's often in crisis," she said. "Learning how to hold space for that, while also managing complex medical equipment, takes real practice."

## Regional Ripple Effects

Bellin Health's NICU serves as a referral center for smaller hospitals across northeastern Wisconsin that lack the capability to care for premature or critically ill newborns. A more stable, well-trained nursing staff at the regional NICU has downstream effects on those smaller facilities, which depend on Bellin's unit having capacity to accept transfers when local hospitals cannot safely manage a complex delivery.

Hospital leaders say early signs from the first residency cohort are encouraging, with retention rates among participating nurses tracking well above the unit's historical average for new hires. They attribute the difference to the extended mentorship period, which gives new nurses more time to build confidence before working independently during a night shift with a full patient assignment.

## Looking Ahead

Bellin Health officials say they hope to expand the residency model to other specialty units, including pediatric intensive care, if the NICU program continues to show strong retention results. For a health system serving a region with its own share of rural and small-town hospitals, building a homegrown pipeline of specialized nurses may prove to be one of the more durable answers to a staffing challenge that shows few signs of easing on its own.
