Dr. Renee Okafor spent more than a decade practicing as a family physician before deciding to build something outside the conventional clinic model. This spring, she opened Harborlight Wellness Collective a few blocks from downtown Kenosha, a practice that brings together acupuncture, guided meditation, nutrition counseling, and functional health coaching under a single roof.

"I got tired of seeing patients for seven-minute appointments and referring them out to five different specialists who never talked to each other," Okafor said. "This is my attempt to build something more integrated, where the practitioners are actually in conversation about a person's whole picture."

The collective currently employs four practitioners alongside Okafor: a licensed acupuncturist, a registered dietitian, a meditation and breathwork instructor, and a massage therapist specializing in myofascial release. Okafor said the goal is for clients to move between services as part of a coordinated plan rather than booking each modality as an isolated appointment.

## Filling a Regional Gap

Kenosha, positioned between the larger wellness markets of Milwaukee and Chicago, has historically had fewer integrative health options than either neighboring metro area, according to Okafor, who said many of her patients previously drove forty-five minutes or more to access services like acupuncture. She said that travel barrier was part of what convinced her the city could support a dedicated collective.

"People were already spending the money and the time to get this kind of care," Okafor said. "They were just doing it in Chicago or in Milwaukee. I wanted to make the case that it could exist here too, serving this community specifically."

Early client intake data, which Okafor shared informally, suggests a client base weighted toward people managing chronic conditions like autoimmune disorders and persistent stress-related symptoms, many of whom were referred by local primary care physicians who lack in-house options for complementary care.

## Balancing Holistic Practice With Clinical Rigor

Okafor said she has been deliberate about avoiding some of the credibility issues that have dogged the broader wellness industry, requiring all practitioners at Harborlight to hold state licensure or accredited certification in their respective fields and steering clear of services not supported by at least some clinical research.

"There's a lot of noise in this space, and some of it verges into things I'm not comfortable putting my name behind," Okafor said. "Everything we offer here has to meet a bar where I can explain to a skeptical physician colleague why it's on our menu."

Acupuncturist Soo-Jin Park, who joined the practice after nine years in private practice in Chicago, said the collaborative structure has changed how she works. "I used to operate almost entirely in isolation from a patient's other providers," Park said. "Here, if a client is also seeing our dietitian, we can actually coordinate, which changes what I recommend in a session."

Okafor said Harborlight is in the process of applying to accept a limited number of insurance plans for acupuncture services, a slow-moving process she expects to take much of the coming year, but one she considers essential to making the practice accessible beyond clients who can pay entirely out of pocket.
