Ingrid Solberg trained in classical facial technique at estheticism schools in Vienna and Budapest before returning to her native Milwaukee to open Cream City Facial Bar, a compact studio tucked between a coffee roaster and a design gallery on Milwaukee Street in the Historic Third Ward. The techniques she brought back are old-world, but the ingredients, she decided early on, would be distinctly Wisconsin.

"European facial traditions are built around using what's regionally available, that's actually the whole philosophy," Solberg said. "So it felt strange to import that tradition here and then still use the same imported ingredients everyone else uses. I wanted to actually apply the principle, not just the technique."

That principle now shows up throughout Cream City's treatment menu, from a buttermilk and honey enzyme mask sourced from a dairy near Waterloo to an apple-based exfoliating treatment using fruit from a Door County orchard during harvest season. Solberg said she rotates several treatments seasonally based on ingredient availability, a practice more common in European spa traditions than in most American facial bars.

## Reviving a Slower Style of Facial Work

Much of the current American facial market has trended toward fast, high-tech treatments, incorporating devices like microcurrent tools and LED masks promising visible results within a single session. Cream City deliberately positions itself in contrast to that trend, offering a signature 75-minute treatment built around extended manual massage technique, a hallmark of the classical European approach Solberg studied abroad.

"A lot of clients come in expecting a fifteen-minute service with a machine doing most of the work," Solberg said. "I have to explain that what we do is closer to the version of a facial that existed before all of that technology, and that the massage work itself is doing real lymphatic and circulatory work, not just feeling nice."

Solberg said the extended manual technique requires more physical stamina from estheticians than device-assisted treatments, prompting her to limit each practitioner to a maximum of five full treatments per day to avoid repetitive strain injuries common in the profession.

## Building a Wisconsin-Sourced Supply Chain

Sourcing regionally has proven more logistically complex than Solberg initially anticipated, requiring relationships with small dairies, orchards, and a Third Ward honey producer willing to supply consistent small-batch quantities suited to a spa rather than a restaurant or grocery buyer. She said several early supplier relationships fell through over minimum order requirements before she found partners willing to work at her scale.

"Most food producers around here are set up to sell to restaurants or farmers markets, not spas," Solberg said. "I had to explain what I needed more than once before people understood I was serious."

Client Fatima Nasser, a Third Ward resident who has become a regular at Cream City since it opened, said the sourcing story adds something to the experience beyond the treatment itself. "It's a nice thing to know when you're lying there, that the mask on your face came from a farm forty minutes away rather than a warehouse somewhere," Nasser said.

Solberg said she plans to expand the seasonal ingredient rotation further into the fall, working with a Kenosha County apple orchard on a treatment timed to the harvest, and is exploring a partnership with a regional flower farm for a rosewater-based summer line next year.
