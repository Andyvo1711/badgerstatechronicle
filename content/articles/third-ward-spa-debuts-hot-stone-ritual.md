On a January afternoon when the wind off Lake Michigan cuts straight through the cobblestone streets of the Historic Third Ward, the warm, low-lit treatment rooms at Lakeshore Stone & Spa feel like a different climate entirely. The nine-year-old spa on North Broadway has spent the past several months developing what owner Elena Voss calls its "Harbor Ritual," a 90-minute hot stone treatment that borrows its pacing and imagery from the lake just blocks away.

"We wanted something that felt distinctly Milwaukee, not something you could get at any spa in any city," Voss said. "The stones are heated in stages, the way the lake temperature actually shifts through a day, and the massage sequence follows that same slow build and release."

The treatment uses basalt stones sourced from a supplier in northern Minnesota, chosen for their density and heat retention, paired with a warmed juniper and cedar oil blend meant to evoke the North Woods rather than the more common eucalyptus. Voss said the idea came out of client feedback collected over two years of comment cards, many of which asked for something that felt more rooted in place.

## A Response to Winter Fatigue

January and February have historically been slow months for spas across Wisconsin, and Lakeshore Stone & Spa is no exception. Voss said bookings typically dip 20 to 30 percent after the holiday rush, a pattern echoed by other Third Ward business owners who describe a post-holiday lull that stretches into March.

The Harbor Ritual was designed in part to counter that seasonal dip. Since its soft launch in early January, the treatment has accounted for nearly a third of all massage bookings at the spa, according to internal figures Voss shared. Staff have also noticed a shift in who is booking: more first-time clients, many arriving via referrals from local hotels near the Milwaukee Public Market.

"People are looking for a reason to leave the house in January," said lead massage therapist Odalys Fenn, who helped design the stone-heating sequence. "If we can give them something that feels like an event, not just a maintenance appointment, that changes the whole conversation."

## Training and Safety Considerations

Introducing a new hot stone protocol required additional certification hours for the spa's five massage therapists, each of whom completed an advanced thermotherapy course before the treatment's debut. Wisconsin does not mandate a specific hot stone certification beyond a therapist's general massage therapy license, but Voss said she wanted her team trained to a higher standard given the treatment's reliance on precise stone temperatures.

"Burns are the obvious risk with any hot stone work, so we built in redundant temperature checks," Voss said. Stones are monitored with an infrared thermometer between uses and never placed directly on skin without a barrier of oil and, for sensitive areas, a thin cloth.

The spa has also begun offering a seasonal variant, the "Thaw," aimed at clients recovering from winter sports injuries common in the area, from Nordic skiing at Lapham Peak to pond hockey leagues around Bay View. That version incorporates deeper tissue work along the calves and shoulders, areas Fenn said are most commonly strained by cold-weather activity.

Voss said she plans to evaluate the Harbor Ritual's performance through the spring before deciding whether to expand it into a full seasonal menu, with ideas already circulating for a summer treatment built around the Third Ward's farmers market season.
