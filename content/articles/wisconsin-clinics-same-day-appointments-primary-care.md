Primary care clinics across Wisconsin have begun setting aside a portion of each day's appointment schedule specifically for same-day requests, a scheduling change aimed at easing what many patients describe as an increasingly frustrating wait for routine and semi-urgent care. The shift comes as the state continues to grapple with a shortage of primary care physicians that has left some patients waiting weeks for a standard appointment.

Under the new scheduling model, adopted in varying forms by clinics affiliated with several of Wisconsin's larger health systems, roughly fifteen to twenty percent of daily appointment slots are held open until the morning of the appointment day, reserved for patients calling with symptoms that need attention sooner than a routine follow-up but don't necessarily require an emergency room visit.

## A Response to Growing Wait Times

State workforce data has shown a widening gap between the number of practicing primary care physicians and Wisconsin's overall population growth, particularly pronounced outside the state's largest metro areas. A clinic operations director involved in implementing the same-day model said the change grew out of direct patient complaints about scheduling delays.

"We kept hearing the same frustration in patient satisfaction surveys: people couldn't get in when they were actually sick," the operations director said. "Reserving same-day capacity doesn't add more physicians to the schedule, but it does let us prioritize acute needs without making every patient wait as long for routine care."

Clinics using the model report that same-day slots typically fill by mid-morning on busy days, particularly during cold and flu season, suggesting strong underlying demand for the option even as it requires careful balancing against routine visit availability.

## Triage Nurses Play a Central Role

Making the same-day model work effectively depends heavily on triage nurses, who field incoming calls and determine which patients genuinely need to be seen that day versus those who can be scheduled for a routine visit or referred elsewhere, such as an urgent care clinic or pharmacy-based service for minor issues.

A triage nurse at a Wisconsin primary care clinic said the role requires careful judgment. "You're trying to protect those same-day slots for people who really need them, while also not turning away someone who's genuinely worried and needs reassurance or guidance," she said. "It's a balancing act every single day."

## Reducing Emergency Department Overflow

Health system leaders say the same-day appointment push is also motivated by a desire to reduce unnecessary emergency department visits. Patients who cannot get a timely primary care appointment sometimes turn to the emergency room for conditions that could be managed in a clinic setting, contributing to overcrowding and higher costs for both patients and the health system.

A hospital administrator overseeing several clinics noted that early data suggests a modest reduction in low-acuity emergency department visits at sites with the same-day model in place, though he cautioned that isolating the effect from other contributing factors remains difficult.

## Not a Complete Solution

Physicians and clinic administrators are careful to note that same-day scheduling adjustments address symptoms of the primary care shortage rather than its underlying cause. Wisconsin continues to face a long-term need for more primary care physicians and advanced practice providers, particularly in rural and underserved urban areas, a challenge that scheduling changes alone cannot resolve.

Still, for patients dealing with a sudden illness or a worsening chronic condition, the ability to get an appointment within hours rather than weeks represents a meaningful, if partial, improvement in access to care that many Wisconsin clinics are now working to sustain.
