Camille Odegaard had a problem that many spa owners would recognize but few manage to solve: her treatments were consistently well-reviewed, her therapists were skilled, and yet client retention at Bluff City Day Spa near downtown La Crosse had plateaued for two straight years. The issue, she eventually concluded, wasn't the quality of the massages or facials themselves. It was everything happening around them.

"People weren't leaving bad reviews about the actual service," Odegaard said. "They were leaving lukewarm reviews about scheduling confusion, or feeling rushed at checkout, or not knowing what to expect before their first visit. Death by a thousand small frustrations."

Over the past year, Odegaard has rebuilt much of the spa's client experience from the ground up, starting with the earliest touchpoint most clients encounter: the online booking system. The spa switched software platforms to reduce double-bookings and added automated but personalized pre-appointment messages describing what a first-time client should expect, down to details like the temperature of the relaxation lounge and whether they'll need to change clothes for a given treatment.

## Rethinking the Welcome

Odegaard said one of the most impactful changes was almost embarrassingly simple: training front desk staff to greet returning clients by referencing something specific from their last visit, whether that was a preference for a particular scent of massage oil or a note about muscle tension in a specific area.

"We started keeping better internal notes and actually using them," Odegaard said. "It sounds obvious, but most spas, including mine before this, don't consistently do it. Clients notice immediately when you remember them versus when you're treating every visit like their first."

The spa also restructured its checkout process to eliminate what Odegaard described as an awkward, rushed transition from a relaxed treatment straight into a transactional payment conversation. Clients are now offered a few minutes in the relaxation lounge with herbal tea before being walked through payment and rebooking, a small buffer Odegaard said has measurably improved end-of-visit satisfaction scores collected through post-appointment surveys.

## Measuring What Actually Matters

Rather than relying solely on online reviews, which Odegaard said tend to skew toward extreme experiences in either direction, Bluff City Day Spa now sends a short, structured survey after every visit, tracking specific metrics like perceived wait time, staff attentiveness, and clarity of pricing separately from overall satisfaction.

"General satisfaction scores can hide a lot," Odegaard said. "You can have someone who loved their massage but still won't come back because the whole experience around it felt disorganized. We wanted to catch that.”

Since implementing the changes, Odegaard said client retention over a rolling twelve-month period has improved by roughly eighteen percent, based on internal booking data she has tracked since the overhaul began. Longtime esthetician Renata Kowalski, who has worked at the spa for six years, said the shift has changed the atmosphere for staff as well as clients.

"There's less scrambling now, less apologizing for things that were out of our control before," Kowalski said. "When the systems around us work better, it's honestly easier to do good work in the treatment room too."

Odegaard said she now spends as much time reviewing operational workflows as she does evaluating treatment menus, a reallocation of attention she considers overdue for an industry that she said too often treats client experience as secondary to technical skill.
