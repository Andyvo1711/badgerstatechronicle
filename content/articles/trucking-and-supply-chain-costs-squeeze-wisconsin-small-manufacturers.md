Small and mid-sized Wisconsin manufacturers say elevated trucking and freight costs continue to squeeze profit margins, even as other supply chain disruptions that defined recent years have largely eased. Unlike the acute shortages and delays that characterized prior disruptions, the current challenge is more straightforwardly a cost problem, one that smaller manufacturers describe as particularly difficult to pass along to customers in competitive markets.

Freight represents a meaningful cost for manufacturers across Wisconsin, a state whose manufacturing base is spread across smaller cities and rural communities rather than concentrated in dense industrial corridors with easy access to multiple transportation modes. That geography means many Wisconsin manufacturers rely heavily on trucking for both inbound raw materials and outbound finished goods, leaving them more exposed to trucking cost fluctuations than manufacturers based in denser coastal logistics hubs with greater access to rail and port alternatives.

## Diesel Prices and Driver Wages Drive Costs

Diesel fuel costs remain one of the most significant and volatile components of trucking expenses, and fluctuations in fuel prices flow through to shipping rates relatively quickly given the thin margins under which many trucking companies operate. Manufacturers describe diesel price volatility as one of the more difficult variables to plan around, since it can shift meaningfully within a single fiscal quarter, complicating cost projections for shipping-intensive businesses.

Driver wages have also risen as trucking companies compete for a limited pool of qualified commercial drivers, a dynamic that mirrors broader skilled labor shortages across Wisconsin's economy. These wage increases, layered on top of fuel cost volatility, have pushed overall freight rates higher even as some of the acute capacity shortages of recent years have eased.

## Smaller Manufacturers Face Disproportionate Impact

Larger manufacturers with greater shipping volumes generally have more negotiating leverage with freight carriers and can sometimes secure more favorable contract rates or explore alternative transportation modes like rail for high-volume routes. Smaller manufacturers, by contrast, often lack the shipping volume to negotiate meaningfully favorable rates and can face difficulty finding carriers willing to prioritize smaller, less consistent shipment volumes.

"When you're shipping a few pallets at a time rather than full truckloads every week, you just don't have the same leverage that a bigger operation does," said a logistics coordinator at a small Wisconsin metal fabrication company, describing the challenge of managing freight costs without the shipping volume needed to negotiate preferential rates. Some smaller manufacturers have turned to freight brokers or third-party logistics providers to help aggregate shipping volume and access better rates, though these services come with their own cost considerations.

## Strategies to Manage Rising Costs

Wisconsin manufacturers have adopted a range of strategies to manage elevated freight costs, including consolidating shipments to reduce total trip frequency, renegotiating supplier contracts to shift more freight responsibility upstream, and in some cases relocating supplier relationships closer to their own facilities to reduce shipping distances entirely. Regional sourcing, buying from suppliers located within Wisconsin or neighboring states rather than more distant national or international suppliers, has gained renewed attention as one lever manufacturers can pull to manage transportation costs, even when unit prices from more distant suppliers might otherwise be lower.

Some manufacturers have also explored intermodal shipping options that combine rail and truck transport for longer-distance shipments, though this option generally only makes economic sense for larger shipment volumes and specific route geographies where rail infrastructure access is convenient.

## An Ongoing Cost Management Challenge

Industry groups representing Wisconsin manufacturers describe freight and logistics costs as likely to remain an ongoing management challenge rather than a temporary disruption that will simply resolve itself. For smaller manufacturers operating on already thin margins, effectively managing these costs increasingly requires the same level of strategic attention historically reserved for raw material sourcing and labor costs, reflecting how central logistics has become to overall manufacturing competitiveness in today's economic environment.
