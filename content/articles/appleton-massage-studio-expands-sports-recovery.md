When Greg Halvorsen opened Fox Valley Muscle & Motion five years ago in a modest two-room space near downtown Appleton, he expected a client base mostly made up of office workers dealing with desk-related tension. Instead, he found himself building relationships with cross-country coaches, physical therapists, and youth hockey parents from across the Fox Cities.

That demand has now pushed the studio to expand from three treatment rooms to six, a move Halvorsen said was overdue by at least a year. "We were turning away same-week appointments almost every week, especially in the lead-up to race season," he said.

## Athletes Driving a Different Kind of Demand

The Fox Cities Marathon, run each September through Appleton, Neenah, and Menasha, has become something of an annual stress test for the studio's booking calendar. Halvorsen said the weeks surrounding the race reliably produce a spike in requests for pre-event loosening sessions and post-race deep tissue work, a pattern that has repeated and intensified every year since the studio opened.

But marathon season is only part of the picture. Halvorsen said a steadier, less predictable source of growth has come from parents of youth athletes competing in club soccer, hockey, and gymnastics throughout the Fox Valley, many of whom are now bringing teenagers in for maintenance massage rather than waiting until an injury forces the issue.

"Ten years ago, a fifteen-year-old getting a sports massage would have been unusual," said massage therapist Priya Osei, who joined the studio in its second year. "Now I have a standing Tuesday client who's a sixteen-year-old goalkeeper, and her mom books it the same way she'd book a physical therapy appointment."

## Building Out Specialized Services

The expansion allowed Fox Valley Muscle & Motion to add equipment previously out of reach for the smaller space, including a percussive therapy station and a dedicated stretching area where clients can work through assisted mobility exercises before or after a massage session. Halvorsen said the studio also brought on a fourth therapist with a background in orthopedic rehabilitation to handle more complex injury cases in coordination with local physical therapy clinics.

Insurance coverage remains a persistent complication. Wisconsin does not require insurers to cover massage therapy outside of specific medical referral situations, which means most sessions at the studio are still paid out of pocket. Halvorsen said he's seen a modest but growing number of clients using health savings account funds to cover recovery-focused appointments, a shift he attributes to broader awareness of massage as a legitimate recovery tool rather than an occasional indulgence.

"We're not a spa in the traditional sense, even though people sometimes expect that when they walk in," Halvorsen said. "Most of what happens in this building looks more like physical therapy with a different name."

With the expanded space now fully staffed, Halvorsen said his next goal is building a formal partnership with at least one Fox Cities high school athletic department to offer discounted recovery sessions for student athletes during peak competition seasons, a conversation he said is already underway with administrators.
