School districts across Wisconsin are in the middle of a messy, uneven process of figuring out how artificial intelligence tools belong in K-12 classrooms, with policies varying widely from district to district and, in some cases, from school to school within the same district. Administrators describe a balancing act between genuine excitement about AI's potential to personalize instruction and very real anxiety about plagiarism, screen time, and the widening gap between districts with resources to invest in training and those without.

Several larger districts have begun piloting AI-powered tutoring software in math and reading, tools that adjust difficulty in real time based on student responses and can flag struggling students for teacher intervention earlier than traditional testing cycles might catch them. Smaller and more rural districts, however, often lack both the technology budget and the dedicated technology staff to evaluate and roll out similar tools responsibly.

## A Patchwork of Policies

"Right now it really depends on which district, and honestly sometimes which building, a student is in," said a technology integration specialist who works with multiple districts in the Fox Valley region. "Some schools have detailed AI use policies for teachers and students. Others are basically operating on old cell phone policies that were never written with this in mind."

At the high school level, concerns have centered heavily on written assignments, as AI writing tools have made it significantly easier for students to generate essays that can be difficult for teachers to distinguish from original student work. Several districts have responded by shifting more writing assessment into the classroom, using in-person, handwritten, or closely supervised formats rather than take-home essays.

## Piloting Personalized Learning Tools

On the more optimistic side, elementary and middle school teachers piloting AI-assisted tutoring software report early enthusiasm, particularly for its ability to provide immediate feedback on math problems, something that's difficult for a single teacher to do consistently across a full classroom of students working at different paces.

"For a kid who's stuck on a concept, waiting until tomorrow for feedback can mean they've already moved on to something else feeling defeated," said an elementary math teacher piloting one of the new tools. "Immediate, adaptive feedback has genuinely changed how some of my students engage with math homework."

## Equity Concerns

Education advocates have raised concerns that the uneven rollout of AI tools risks widening existing gaps between well-resourced districts and those serving lower-income or rural communities, where broadband access and device availability can already be inconsistent. Some worry that AI literacy itself could become a new dividing line, with students in well-funded districts graduating comfortable and skilled with these tools while others are left unprepared.

"We talk a lot about not wanting AI to replace teaching, and I agree with that," said a rural district superintendent. "But I also worry about our kids showing up to college or the workforce having never used these tools at all, while kids from wealthier districts have had years of guided practice."

## State-Level Guidance Still Developing

State education officials have offered general guidance on AI use in schools but have largely left specific policy decisions to individual districts, a decentralized approach consistent with Wisconsin's traditionally local control model for public education. Several district administrators interviewed said they would welcome clearer statewide standards, particularly around data privacy for AI tools that collect student information, even as they value the flexibility to adapt policies to their own community's needs.
