A newly launched statewide mentorship program is pairing early-career teachers in Wisconsin with veteran educators, including some from outside their own districts, in an effort to address a well-documented problem in education: a large share of new teachers leave the profession within their first five years, often citing lack of support as much as workload or pay. Organizers say the cross-district structure is meant to give new teachers access to experienced mentors even in smaller districts that may not have enough veteran staff internally to run a robust mentoring program on their own.

The program, developed through a partnership between several teacher preparation programs and regional education service agencies, matches participants based on subject area and grade level rather than geography, allowing a new elementary teacher in a small rural district, for example, to be paired with a veteran teacher from a larger suburban district who teaches the same grade.

## Addressing Early-Career Attrition

"The first few years of teaching are brutal for almost everyone, no matter how well-prepared you feel coming out of your program," said an administrator involved in designing the mentorship initiative. "A lot of the teachers we lose aren't leaving because they're bad at the job. They're leaving because they feel isolated and unsupported, especially in their first year or two."

Mentors and mentees communicate through a combination of virtual check-ins, occasional in-person meetups, and a shared online platform where new teachers can ask questions ranging from classroom management strategies to how to navigate difficult conversations with parents. Organizers say the structure was deliberately designed to be flexible, recognizing that both mentors and mentees are already managing demanding full-time teaching schedules.

## Early Feedback

Participants in the program's initial cohort describe it as filling a gap that existing school-based induction programs, which vary widely in quality and consistency across the state, don't always address. "My building mentor was great, but she taught a completely different subject and grade level than me," said a first-year high school science teacher paired with a mentor through the new program. "Having someone who actually teaches the same content, even if they're in a different district, made the advice so much more specific and useful."

Veteran teachers serving as mentors say they've found the experience valuable as well, describing it as a form of professional development in its own right. "Mentoring forces you to articulate things you do automatically after years in the classroom," said a veteran middle school teacher serving as a mentor. "It's made me more intentional about my own practice, honestly."

## Funding and Expansion Plans

The program is currently funded through a mix of state education grants and contributions from participating regional education agencies, with organizers hoping to demonstrate strong enough retention outcomes among participants to justify expanded, more permanent funding. Early data collection is focused on tracking whether mentored teachers remain in the profession, and in their initial districts, at higher rates than statewide averages.

## A Broader Retention Strategy

Education officials involved in the initiative are careful to describe mentorship as one piece of a larger set of strategies needed to address teacher retention, alongside compensation, workload, and administrative support. Still, they say early anecdotal feedback has been encouraging enough to pursue expansion, with hopes of roughly doubling the number of participating teacher pairs in the coming school year if funding allows.
