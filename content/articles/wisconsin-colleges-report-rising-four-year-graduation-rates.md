Several Wisconsin colleges and universities report improvements in their four-year graduation rates, a metric that has drawn increased attention nationally as students and families weigh the cost of higher education against the risk of accumulating extra tuition and debt from spending five or six years to complete what is nominally a four-year degree. Administrators credit a mix of proactive academic advising, streamlined degree requirements, and better early-warning systems for identifying students at risk of falling behind.

For years, a significant share of students at four-year institutions nationally, including in Wisconsin, have taken longer than four years to graduate, often due to switching majors, needing to retake courses, or struggling to get into required classes that filled up before they could register. Extra semesters translate directly into extra cost, both in tuition and in lost earning years, making on-time graduation an increasingly central focus for university administrators under pressure to demonstrate value to increasingly cost-conscious families.

## Proactive Advising Systems

"We used to wait for students to come to us with problems," said a director of academic advising at a Wisconsin public university that has seen graduation rate improvements in recent years. "Now we're using data to flag students who might be off track well before it becomes a crisis, whether that's a student who hasn't declared a major by a certain point or someone whose course load doesn't add up to graduating on time."

These early-warning systems typically draw on data like course completion patterns, credit accumulation pace, and academic performance to flag students for additional advising outreach. Administrators say the goal is to catch potential delays early enough that students still have time to adjust their course plans without adding an extra semester or year.

## Streamlining Degree Requirements

Several universities have also reviewed and simplified degree requirements in recent years, eliminating redundant or rarely necessary coursework that had accumulated over decades of incremental curriculum changes. Officials say some degree programs had grown unnecessarily complex over time, with students sometimes needing to navigate confusing prerequisite chains that made efficient scheduling difficult.

"When you actually sit down and map out some of these old degree requirements, you find courses that were added for reasons nobody quite remembers, creating bottlenecks that serve no real academic purpose," said a provost's office administrator involved in a recent curriculum review. "Cleaning that up isn't about lowering standards. It's about removing unnecessary friction."

## Persistent Challenges

Despite the gains, administrators acknowledge that not all delays are within a university's control. Students working substantial hours at outside jobs, those with caregiving responsibilities, and transfer students navigating credit transfer complications continue to face graduation timelines outside the university's direct influence. Several institutions say they're working on improving transfer credit policies specifically, an area that has historically caused significant delays for students moving between Wisconsin's technical colleges and four-year institutions.

## What the Data Shows

While individual institutional gains vary, several Wisconsin universities have reported multi-percentage-point increases in four-year graduation rates over the past several admissions cycles. Officials caution against reading too much into any single year's numbers, given that cohort sizes and other variables can shift results, but describe the overall trend as a positive sign that targeted interventions are having a measurable effect.

University leaders say they plan to continue investing in advising infrastructure and curriculum review, framing on-time graduation as both a student success issue and an increasingly important factor in how prospective students and families evaluate which college offers the best value.
